Hypothesis Testing Explorer

Five guided lessons on the logic, methods, and limits of statistical hypothesis testing.

Null & Alternative
H₀ is the "no effect" claim. H₁ is what you're trying to demonstrate. You never prove H₁ — you only decide whether the data are inconsistent with H₀.
Test Statistic
A single number summarizing how far the sample is from what H₀ predicts, measured in standard errors. z = (x̄ − μ₀)/(σ/√n), or t with estimated σ.
Critical Value Method
Look up the cutoff in a table. If the test statistic exceeds it, reject H₀. The cutoff is set so that only α% of results cross it when H₀ is true.
p-value Method
Compute the probability of a result this extreme or more extreme, assuming H₀. If p < α, reject. Both methods always give the same decision — they are equivalent.
Power & Errors
α = P(reject H₀ | H₀ true) — Type I error. β = P(fail to reject | H₀ false) — Type II error. Power = 1 − β = probability of detecting a true effect.

What This Lab Teaches

  • The logic of hypothesis testing: what the sampling distribution represents and why it's the foundation of every test.
  • Why the critical value method and the p-value method are two framings of the same question — and always agree.
  • How the direction of H₁ (left, right, two-tailed) changes the critical value, the p-value, and possibly the decision.
  • The Type I / Type II trade-off and why increasing sample size improves power without inflating α.

Full Reference — Hypothesis Testing

The Sampling Distribution

The entire framework of hypothesis testing rests on one idea: if H₀ were true, what distribution would our test statistic follow? For a one-sample z-test, the answer is the standard normal N(0,1). For a t-test, it's Student's t with df = n−1. The test statistic measures how many standard errors the sample is from H₀'s prediction.

z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ / √n)   [known σ]
t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n)   [estimated σ, df = n−1]

Critical Value Method — "The Table Method"

Before computing anything, look up the critical value for your α level, test type, and direction. This is the value that the test statistic must exceed to reject H₀.

  • Two-tailed, α=.05: z* = ±1.960 (z); varies with df (t)
  • Right-tailed, α=.05: z* = 1.645 (z)
  • Left-tailed, α=.05: z* = −1.645 (z)

Decision rule: Reject H₀ if the test statistic is in the rejection region (past the critical value). The rejection region is the α% most extreme area of the null distribution.

p-value Method

The p-value is the probability of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one obtained, assuming H₀ is true. It is the area under the null distribution in the tail(s) defined by the observed test statistic.

Decision rule: Reject H₀ if p < α. The two methods always agree because they answer the same question with different units: "did we cross the threshold?"

Right-tailed:  p = P(Z ≥ z_obs)
Left-tailed:   p = P(Z ≤ z_obs)
Two-tailed:   p = 2 · P(Z ≥ |z_obs|)

z-test vs t-test

Use a z-test when the population standard deviation σ is known (rare in practice). Use a t-test when you estimate σ from the sample using s. The t-distribution has heavier tails than the normal — it accounts for the additional uncertainty in estimating σ. As df → ∞, t → z.

The t-distribution's shape depends on degrees of freedom (df = n−1). At df=5 the tails are visibly heavier; at df=30+ it is nearly indistinguishable from the standard normal.

One-tailed vs Two-tailed Tests

The direction of H₁ must be specified before seeing the data. A two-tailed test asks "is the true mean different from μ₀ in either direction?" and spreads α across both tails. A one-tailed test concentrates all of α in one tail, giving more power in that direction.

Two-tailed
H₁: μ ≠ μ₀. Use when you have no prior directional hypothesis. More conservative — harder to reject H₀.
One-tailed
H₁: μ > μ₀ or μ < μ₀. Use only when direction is theoretically motivated before data collection.

Important: Switching from two-tailed to one-tailed after seeing the data to achieve significance is p-hacking — a serious methodological error.

Type I and Type II Errors

Type I Error (α)
Reject H₀ when H₀ is actually true. A "false positive." Rate is exactly α when all assumptions hold.
Type II Error (β)
Fail to reject H₀ when H₁ is true. A "false negative." Rate depends on effect size, n, and α.

Power = 1 − β. Increasing n is the most reliable way to increase power without increasing α. Increasing α also increases power but at the cost of more false positives.

Common Misconceptions

  • "p < .05 means the result is important." No — significance depends on n. A trivial effect becomes significant with enough data.
  • "p = .06 means the result is false." No — p-values are continuous. p = .06 and p = .04 are barely distinguishable.
  • "Fail to reject means H₀ is true." No — it means the data are insufficient to reject it. Absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence.
  • "The p-value is the probability H₀ is true." No — it is P(data this extreme | H₀ is true), not P(H₀ true | data).

The Four Decision Outcomes

✓ Correct Rejection
Reject H₀ when H₁ is truly correct. Probability = Power = 1 − β.
✓ Correct Retention
Fail to reject H₀ when H₀ is truly correct. Probability = 1 − α.
✗ Type I Error
Reject H₀ when H₀ is truly correct. Probability = α.
✗ Type II Error
Fail to reject H₀ when H₁ is truly correct. Probability = β.
