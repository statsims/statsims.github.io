Bayesian vs. Frequentist — Statistics Lab

Build intuition for two fundamentally different answers to the question: what does "probability" mean?

P-value
P(data | H₀) — How surprising is our data assuming the null is true? Not a probability about the hypothesis.
Prior P(θ)
Bayesian belief about the parameter θ before observing data. Encoded as a probability distribution.
Posterior P(θ|data)
Updated belief after data: Posterior ∝ Likelihood × Prior. The Bayesian answer to inference.
Confidence Interval
A procedure with 95% coverage: 95% of all such intervals will contain θ. Not a probability about θ.
Credible Interval
P(θ ∈ interval | data) = 95%. A direct probability statement about the parameter — what people want.

The Core Intuitions

  • Frequentist treats data as random — imagine repeating the experiment many times. Parameters are fixed unknowns.
  • Bayesian treats parameters as random — they have distributions representing uncertainty. Data is fixed once observed.
  • Frequentist output: a decision (reject/fail to reject) + a p-value. No probability on hypotheses.
  • Bayesian output: a full posterior distribution. Direct probability statements about parameters.
  • With large n, both approaches usually agree. Small n and strong priors is where they diverge most.

The Classic Misconceptions

  • "p < .05 means H₀ has <5% chance of being true." Wrong — that requires a prior (Bayes' theorem).
  • "95% CI means 95% probability θ is inside." Wrong — the CI either covers θ or it doesn't. The 95% is about the long-run procedure, not any single interval.
  • "Failing to reject H₀ means it's true." Wrong — absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence.
  • All three of these sound Bayesian. Only Bayesian inference can actually make these statements.

Full Reference — Bayesian vs. Frequentist Inference

Frequentist Inference

Philosophical stance: Probability = long-run relative frequency. A parameter either has a value or it doesn't — it cannot have a probability distribution.

The question asked: P(data as extreme as observed | H₀ is true). If this probability is small, the data are surprising under H₀ — we reject it.

p-value = P(|X − n·p₀| ≥ |k − n·p₀| | H₀: p = p₀)

Confidence interval: Constructed so that 95% of all intervals built this way will contain θ. For any single experiment, the interval either does or doesn't contain θ — no probability applies to it.

What it cannot do: Assign a probability to a hypothesis being true or false. Cannot make statements like "there's a 73% chance the drug works."

The Binomial Test (Coin Flip)

H₀: p = 0.5 (fair coin). Two-sided p-value for k heads in n flips:

p = P(X ≤ min(k, n−k)) + P(X ≥ max(k, n−k))
where X ~ Binomial(n, 0.5)

Wilson confidence interval for proportions:

CI = (p̂ + z²/2n ± z√(p̂(1−p̂)/n + z²/4n²)) / (1 + z²/n)

Bayesian Inference

Philosophical stance: Probability = degree of belief. Parameters are uncertain, so they get probability distributions. Updating beliefs rationally is the core operation.

The operation: Use Bayes' rule to update a prior with the likelihood of the data:

P(θ | data) ∝ P(data | θ) · P(θ)
Posterior ∝ Likelihood × Prior

Conjugate prior for coin flips: Beta prior × Binomial likelihood → Beta posterior.

Prior: Beta(α₀, β₀)
Data: k heads, n−k tails
Posterior: Beta(α₀ + k, β₀ + n − k)
Posterior mean: (α₀ + k) / (α₀ + β₀ + n)

Credible interval: The central 95% of the posterior. Directly interpreted as: "Given the data and prior, P(θ ∈ interval) = 95%."

Prior Choices for a Coin

  • Beta(1,1) Uniform: All values of p equally plausible. No prior information.
  • Beta(5,5): Weakly believes coin is fair. Equivalent to having seen 5 heads and 5 tails previously.
  • Beta(20,20): Strongly believes coin is fair. Needs ~40+ flips of strong evidence to shift.
  • Asymmetric priors: Beta(2,5) favors low p; Beta(5,2) favors high p.

With n >> α₀ + β₀ (the "prior sample size"), data overwhelms the prior and the posterior converges to the MLE.
